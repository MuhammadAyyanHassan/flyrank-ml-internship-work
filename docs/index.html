Which pages are about to lose search visibility?

Ranking a content library at decision time so the pages most likely to decline next month rise to the top of a short review queue — tested against a transparent rule and a held-out future month.

Muhammad Ayyan Hassan · FlyRank ML Internship (Machine Learning track) · Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · August 2026

Built on a pseudonymized research dataset. No client names, domains, URLs, or search queries appear anywhere in this paper.

1Abstract

Can a content team, using only search behavior observable at decision time, rank a page library so the pages most likely to lose search visibility next month rise to the top of a short review queue? Working from a pseudonymized FlyRank search-warehouse release of 158,549 page-months across 46 clients, we defined a next-month decline proxy and engineered nine decision-window features covering both traffic level and within-window dynamics. A transparent rule baseline, logistic regression, and gradient boosting were compared under client-grouped cross-validation, scored by Precision@K against the base rate on the identical split, and re-tested on a later, unseen month. At the operational depth of the top 100 pages the logistic model reached 93.0% precision against a 47.8% base rate — while the rule baseline did no better than chance — but an ablation shows most of that lift comes from within-window momentum features, so the system is better described as triaging pages already sliding than as forecasting a decline before it begins. The deliverable is therefore a ranked, reason-coded review queue for prioritizing human attention — a decision-support tool, not a claim about Google’s algorithm or the cause of any page’s decline.

2Introduction & problem statement

A team responsible for a large body of published content — here, an agency managing many client sites — has far more pages than it can ever hand-review. Each month only a small slice can be looked at closely. The practical question is not “what is wrong with the whole library,” but a triage question: which pages should a human open first?

Review a page that was going to stay healthy and you have spent scarce attention for nothing. Miss a page that is about to lose search visibility and traffic erodes before anyone notices. A good review queue is the difference between catching a slide early and finding out from a quarterly report.

This work supports that one decision. The unit of analysis is a single page (for a single client) in a single month; the output is a rank, a reason code, a suggested review action, and a confidence band; the action a person takes is to review the top of the queue downward until their time runs out.

Why involve a model at all? The risk signal is spread thinly across the daily behavior of 158,549 page-months — no one eyeballs that at scale, and eyeballing invites inconsistent, gut-feel prioritization. A model can rank the whole library consistently and cheaply, and — crucially — can be checked against a transparent rule so we know whether it actually earns its complexity. To be clear from the outset about what this is: it prioritizes pages already at risk for human review. It is triage, not a forecast of decline before any sign of it appears, and the results section is built to show exactly where that line falls.

3Data

The source is the pseudonymized FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703, specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table. It was queried in place with DuckDB over the gated Hugging Face release (hf://); the dataset itself is never downloaded into the repository or committed. Only the two calendar months needed for each analysis frame were pulled — not the full 78.8M-row fact table — and the release’s held-out _sample table (which holds the last month of the release) was never touched.

Two time frames

Every page is summarized over a one-month decision window and then checked against the following month, the outcome window:

  • Frame A (development) — March 2026 features → April 2026 outcome. 158,549 rows, 46 clients, decline rate 47.82%. All model development and cross-validation happen here.
  • Frame B (forward-time test) — April 2026 features → May 2026 outcome. 183,345 rows, 51 clients, decline rate 47.21%. Used once, at the end, to check stability on a month the model never saw. 46 clients appear in both frames.

What was excluded, and why

Rows without confirmed Search Console availability (gsc_data_available not true) were dropped. A page also had to appear in both the decision and outcome windows, so that its outcome is defined at all. That second filter is a survivorship rule — pages that vanished entirely between the two months are not counted — and it is named again in the limitations, because it shapes what “decline” can mean here.

On safety: the source carries only pseudonymous hash identifiers, and none are printed in this paper. No client names, domains, URLs, or search queries appear here or in the repository. One data-quality caveat about the average-position column is documented in the limitations section rather than buried here.

4Methodology

The label (a proxy)

A page is labeled a next-month decline (future_decline_label = 1) when it had impressions in the decision window and its outcome-window impressions fell below 80% of the decision-window level. It is a proxy for “lost meaningful visibility,” not a ground-truth verdict, and the 80% cutoff is a modeling choice.

Features — nine, all from the decision window

Every feature is measured strictly inside the decision window and carries a w_ prefix to mark it as decision-time-only. They split into two families:

  • Level (5): w_impressions, w_clicks, w_ctr_pct, w_avg_position (impression-weighted), w_impression_days — how much traffic the page had and how visible it was.
  • Dynamics (4): w_late_share, w_max_day_share, w_impr_cv, w_active_day_share — how that traffic was distributed across the days of the window: late-weighting, single-day spikes, day-to-day variability, and how many days were active at all.

Deliberately excluded to prevent leakage: any outcome-window field or the label itself; the product’s own trend_direction and trend_pct (both label-derived); the product flags health_score, priority_score, and action_type; and both hash identifiers, which are used only to group folds and never as features. As a guardrail, the notebook asserts that every feature in the final feature list carries the w_ decision-window prefix.

Baseline — a transparent rule

The model is measured against a frozen rule from the earlier baseline week, chosen because it is exactly the kind of common-sense heuristic a team would otherwise use by hand: score 5 if a page had at least 500 impressions and an average position of 4–20 and a CTR below the median CTR of that visible band; score 3 for volume alone; 0 otherwise. The band-median CTR is refit inside each training fold only, never on the rows being scored. The per-fold CTR cutoffs were 0.1911%, 0.2155%, 0.1907%, 0.1886%, and 0.1734%.

Models

Two models sit on the same features and the same split: logistic regression (median-impute → standardize → max_iter=1000) as the primary model, and a HistGradientBoostingClassifier as a complexity test — there to answer “does a more flexible model actually do better here?”

Validation

Scoring uses StratifiedGroupKFold (5 folds, shuffled, random_state=42) grouped on client, with out-of-fold predictions and a per-fold assertion that no client appears in both train and test. Every score is therefore earned on clients the model did not train on. Both frames are sorted on their join keys before any split, which makes the run deterministic — an earlier non-determinism from DuckDB’s GROUP BY row order is what made two prior weeks’ AUC numbers disagree, and sorting fixed it.

Uncertainty & the primary metric

Bands come from a 2000-replicate bootstrap (seed 42). They should be read carefully: each band describes how the outcome varies within the selected top-K, with the ranking held fixed — it is not a confidence interval over redrawing the whole population. The primary metric is Precision@K at K = 10, 50, 100, and 500, always reported next to the base rate; the headline operating depth is K = 100, the size of a realistic monthly review queue.

Leakage audit — all ten checks passed

  1. No outcome-window or label-derived column appears among the features.
  2. No hash identifier is used as a model feature.
  3. Every feature carries the decision-window w_ prefix.
  4. The outcome window is strictly later than the decision window.
  5. Exactly one row per client and content key in each frame.
  6. Zero client overlap inside every grouped fold.
  7. The rule’s CTR cutoff is fitted on training rows only, never on evaluation rows.
  8. The forward test uses parameters fitted on Frame A only.
  9. Frames are sorted on join keys before any split, so the run is deterministic.
  10. The exported queue carries no client name, URL, or query field.

Reading the headline

At the top 100 — a realistic monthly review queue — logistic regression flagged pages that declined 93.0% of the time, against a 47.8% base rate. In plain terms, roughly 93 of its top 100 picks were genuine decliners. The rule baseline landed at 43.0%, with a band of [33.0, 53.0] that straddles the base rate: at this depth the common-sense rule is no better than picking at random. Logistic regression’s advantage over the rule clears its bootstrap band, so it is a real separation, not noise.

Did the more complex model earn its keep?

Not at the operating depth. At the top 100, gradient boosting scored 88.0% [81.0, 94.0] versus logistic’s 93.0% [87.0, 98.0] — the bands overlap, so there is no honest case that the heavier model is better where it matters, and the simpler, more transparent logistic model wins by default. The flip side, reported plainly: gradient boosting does pull ahead deeper in the queue (top 500: 93.4% vs 86.8%) and on whole-population ranking quality (ROC-AUC 0.7155 vs 0.6644). If the job were to rank the entire library rather than skim the top 100, gradient boosting would be the pick. For a short triage queue, it is not.

What this test does and does not show

This is a time-forward test, not a client-holdout test. The same 46 clients appear in both months, so it measures whether the model stays stable over time — it does not measure generalization to brand-new clients. That second question is exactly what the client-grouped cross-validation on Frame A answers. And because Frame B has its own base rate (47.21%), its Precision@K is only meaningful against 47.21%, never against Frame A’s 47.82%. The takeaway is narrow and honest: on an unseen later month, the model did not degrade.

6Limitations & honest framing

The one that matters most: this is triage, not a forecast

The ablation showed that adding four within-window dynamics features lifted top-100 precision from 60.0% to 93.0%, and permutation importance put nearly all the weight on w_late_share, w_impr_cv, and w_max_day_share. That lift is real, but it is partly mechanical with the label. The label asks whether next month’s impressions fall below 80% of this month’s; those three features measure whether a page’s impressions were already trailing off, spiking, or unstable inside the decision window. A page visibly sliding by the end of the month tends, by simple momentum, to keep sliding.

This is not leakage — every feature uses decision-window data only, no outcome-window value ever touches the model (the ten-check audit confirms it). But it does mean the honest description is: the model detects pages that are already in decline and are likely to continue, not one that foresees a drop before any sign of it appears. It earns its place as a triage tool that puts in-progress slides at the top of a review queue — nothing on this page claims more than that.

A data-quality caveat on average position

The w_avg_position column contains values below 1.0 (minimum 0.0196; individual examples include 0.116, 0.069, 0.541). A Google average position can never be better than 1.0, so these are an artifact of the pseudonymization applied to the research release, not real rankings. The feature is therefore usable only as a relative signal, not a literal position, and it happens to carry almost no weight in the model anyway (permutation importance −0.00863). Coverage is otherwise near-complete: 157,790 of 158,549 rows are non-null, and the 759 missing values were median-imputed.

What the bootstrap bands do and don’t mean

Each band describes how the decline rate varies within a fixed top-K selection, holding the ranking constant — it is a stability check on the reported precision, not a confidence interval over resampling the whole population. Overlapping bands (as between the two models at the top 100) mean “no honest separation here,” and that is how they are read throughout.

Other boundaries, stated plainly

  • Survivorship. A page must appear in both the decision and outcome windows to have a defined outcome. Pages that vanished entirely between months are not counted, so “decline” here means “still present but down,” never “disappeared.”
  • The label is a proxy with an arbitrary cut. The 80% threshold is a modeling choice; a page at 79% is labeled a decline and one at 81% is not, though little separates them.
  • No causal claim. The data is observational — one summary per page-month, no intervention. Nothing here says that refreshing a page, or any other action, prevents decline. The output supports where to look, not what will happen if you act.
  • One portfolio, three months. Findings come from 46–51 clients of a single agency across three consecutive months of one warehouse release. They do not describe the web at large, and they are not a statement about how Google’s algorithm works or a prediction of it.

7Ranked recommendations

The deliverable is a ranked, reason-coded review queue. Every eligible page is scored by the logistic model’s probability (score_logreg) and sorted high to low; pages with fewer than MIN_ACTIONABLE_IMPRESSIONS = 100 decision-window impressions are set aside as not worth a human’s time. Each surviving row carries a rank, a reason code, a suggested review action, and a confidence band. An editor works the list from the top down until the month’s review budget runs out.

What a FlyRank editor should do, in priority order

  1. Work the top of the queue, not the whole library. The evidence is strongest at shallow depth — ~93% precision at the top 100 on the development frame, holding on the unseen month. Precision decays as you go deeper, so the top 100 is where a fixed review budget buys the most.
  2. Read it as “review first,” not “these will decline.” A high rank means the page is showing the within-window instability associated with continued decline — a reason to open it, not a verdict on its future.
  3. Let the reason code choose the job. A single-day-spike page needs a stability check; a visible-but-low-CTR page needs a title/snippet look. Same queue, different work — the reason code says which.
  4. Respect the confidence band and the Hold override. Lower-confidence rows and pages the no-go rule flagged for Hold should get a human’s judgment before entering any automated workflow.

8Reproducibility

The analysis is deterministic and re-runnable from a fresh clone. Randomness is pinned in two places: random_state=42 for the StratifiedGroupKFold split and np.random.default_rng(42) for the bootstrap. Both frames are sorted on their join keys before any split, which removes the DuckDB row-order non-determinism that made two earlier weeks’ AUC numbers disagree.

Re-running it

  1. Open work/notebooks/capstone.ipynb from the repository in Colab.
  2. Put your Hugging Face token in Colab Secrets as HF_TOKEN — never in the notebook source, never committed. The notebook queries the gated release in place with DuckDB over hf://; the dataset is never downloaded into the repo.
  3. Run top to bottom. The run writes its receipt to work/outputs/capstone_metrics.json, committed alongside the executed notebook so the reported numbers are checkable from the repo rather than taken on faith.
